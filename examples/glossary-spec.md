# Page specification

## Page title
Glossary

## Who this page is for
Students who publish event pages (for a club, an organization or a department) and want to understand AI agents and how they read and use the web. Assume they are smart but new to the topic: they have used ChatGPT or UF NaviGator, but have never heard words like "text extraction" or "agentic traffic." After reading, they should be able to understand every other page on this site without looking anything up.

## What it must contain
A short intro (2-3 sentences) saying what the glossary is for, then these terms. Use the notes to write each definition. Every definition must agree with the page named in brackets, which is where this site already explains the idea.

- **Access failure**: when an AI tool can't reach a page at all, so it answers without having read it. Some models say so; others guess and sound sure. One invented answer placed this UF event at Columbia University. [Method & Limits, Findings]
- **Agent-ready website**: a site designed so AI agents can read it and complete tasks on it reliably. In a controlled study (Elnaffar & Rashidi, 2026), strict task success rose from 49.3% to 89.3% on an agent-ready version, though several changes were bundled together. [Sources]
- **Agentic AI traffic**: visits from AI agents that click, fill out forms and check out. HUMAN Security reported it grew 7,851% in 2025. [The New Reader]
- **AI agent**: unlike a chatbot, which answers from what it already knows, an agent can reason through a problem in steps, browse the live web or your files, and act by clicking, typing, filling out forms or running code. [AI Agents: A Student Guide]
- **AI intermediation (AI-mediated web)**: when an AI reads a page on someone's behalf and passes along what it found, so the person may never see the original page. The central idea of this project. [The New Reader, Home]
- **Alt text**: a written description attached to an image. Good alt text states the facts in the image ("Afternoon workshop moved to Weimer Hall 1064"), not just "Event flyer." It is often the only way an AI reader learns what an image says. [Checklist]
- **Benchmark**: a standard test for comparing AI systems. Mention WebArena (realistic website tasks) and OSWorld (using a whole computer) as examples. [The New Reader]
- **Bots as authors / visitors / readers for us**: this site's three kinds of bots: those that write content, those that crawl or attack sites, and AI assistants that read and act for a person. The third is the fastest-growing kind and the focus of this project. [The New Reader]
- **Browse and verify / Delegate and consume**: two ways people get information. With "browse and verify," people search, click and read sources themselves. With "delegate and consume," they hand the task to an AI and accept its summary. Terms from Lichtenegger, Urman & Hannak (2026). [Sources, Home]
- **Dead Internet Theory**: an online theory, popular since around 2021, that most online activity is now bots and AI. This project's view: the internet isn't dead; it has a new reader. [The New Reader]
- **Deal-breakers**: the conditions that decide whether someone can attend, pays, or gets turned away (deadlines, eligibility, ID, payment). They belong in plain, structured text near the top of a page. [Checklist]
- **Dual-audience web**: the idea that every page now has two audiences, people and AI readers, who fail on different parts of the same page. People miss buried text; AI misses images. [Findings, Home]
- **Generative engine optimization (GEO)**: making content more likely to be used by AI-generated answers, the AI-era counterpart to SEO. Aggarwal et al. (2024) found it raised visibility by up to 40%, but it measures being mentioned, not being described accurately. [Sources]
- **Model Context Protocol (MCP)**: an open standard that lets an AI model connect to outside tools and data, such as a website's editing system, through a defined set of actions. This Glossary page was drafted by an agent that used an MCP server to create it in WordPress. [Not explained elsewhere on this site yet; use this definition as written.]
- **Search engine optimization (SEO)**: shaping pages so search engines rank them well, using keywords and fast pages. It was the main publishing goal of the "search web" era. [The New Reader]
- **Stale copy**: outdated text left on a page after something changed. AI readers trust the words, so in Experiment 2, three of four AI readers sent attendees to the old venue. [Checklist, Findings]
- **Structured content**: information laid out as short labeled lists or explicit rules instead of long paragraphs, like this project's Version B fact sheet. AI answers tended to mirror that structure and flag gaps. [The Experiments, Findings]
- **Text extraction (page loader)**: when a tool converts a web page to plain text before the AI reads it. Anything that exists only in an image, like a flyer, can disappear completely. UF NaviGator's web loader did exactly this in Experiment 2. [Findings, AI Agents: A Student Guide]
- **UF NaviGator**: the University of Florida's AI platform, which gives students free access to roughly 30 models from several companies. All of this project's model tests ran there. [Method & Limits]
- **Verifiability (source support)**: whether each claim in an AI answer is actually backed by the source it cites. Based on Liu, Zhang & Liang (2023); one of the three parts of this project's scoring rubric. [Method & Limits, Sources]

## Structure
- Title, then the short intro.
- Terms in alphabetical order. Each term is a level-3 heading (so both skimming readers and AI readers can find it), followed by a 1-3 sentence definition in plain language.
- Where a term connects to this project's experiments, add one short sentence starting "On this site:" with a link to the relevant page.
- End with a one-sentence note: this page was drafted by an AI agent running on UF NaviGator from my specification, then checked and edited by me, linking to How This Was Built.

## Voice
Match "AI Agents: A Student Guide" and the Checklist: plain, direct, short sentences, practical, no hype. First person singular when referring to the project ("I tested..."). Explain like a knowledgeable classmate, not a textbook.
